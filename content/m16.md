::: objectives
- Take one real problem through seven stages, in order, without skipping the uncomfortable ones
- Write and commit the eval suite before the agent exists
- Set a cost ceiling at stage 1 and enforce it in CI
- Demonstrate graceful failure under a fault you did not choose
- Absorb a mid-project scope change the way real projects require
- Defend the design to a panel, including two questions you know are coming
:::

## Ship one real thing

Everything so far has had scaffolding. Failing tests told you what to build. Test sets came
pre-written. The security lab handed you the attacks.

This removes the scaffolding. You build one agent, for one real problem, over two weeks, and then
explain it to people who will ask hard questions.

If you came in never having written code, look back at F2 for a moment. You are now being asked to
design, build, test, secure and defend a production system. That is a genuine distance, and the
capstone is where you find out you actually travelled it.

::: key
Two students can build the same agent and receive very different grades. Judgement is weighted
highest.
:::

That is not a warning about strictness. It is the actual grading criterion.

An ambitious agent with a weak test suite and an unexamined threat model scores **below** a modest one
whose author can say exactly what it does badly, why they chose this design, and what would change
their mind.

Which is worth saying plainly to anyone who is new here: **you are not being graded on how impressive
the thing is.** You are being graded on whether you know what you built.

## The seven stages, in order

The order is enforced, and the enforcement is the point. Stage 2 before stage 4 is what separates
this from every project that shipped a demo and discovered its failure modes in production.

### 01 · Problem definition — 10%

Who is the user, what is the task, what does success mean, and **why an agent**? Apply the M4
framework in writing: enumerable steps, findings-dependence, value per task, reversibility,
evaluability. Estimate the value of one successful task in money or time.

If the framework says "this should be a workflow", **build the workflow.** Choosing correctly against
your own preference is worth more marks than building the more impressive thing.

### 02 · Eval-first design — 20%

**Written and committed before a line of agent code exists.** At least 30 cases across three tiers,
outcome and trajectory assertions, a written judge rubric, and a validated judge — hand-label 30
outputs, measure kappa, iterate.

The heaviest-weighted stage, deliberately. This is where projects live or die, and doing it first is
the only way to find out that you cannot define success — which is much better to discover in week
one than in week two.

::: jargon Decision Log
A short written record of each significant choice: what you decided, what else you considered, why
you chose it, and **what evidence would make you change your mind.**

That last field is what makes it a decision log rather than a justification. A justification explains
why you were right. A decision log tells the next person — often you, in three months — what would
have made a different answer correct.

This course has been asking for entries since M4. The capstone collects them.
:::

### 03 · Architecture — 15%

A diagram, plus Decision Log entries for: autonomy level (per action class, M13), tool design (M2),
context strategy (M3), memory (M7), single versus multi-agent with the cost arithmetic (M9), and
deployment shape (M15).

Each entry states the decision, the alternatives, the reason, and what evidence would reverse it.
That last field is what makes it a decision log rather than a justification.

```markdown title=decisions/003-single-vs-multi-agent.md
# 003 · Single agent, not orchestrator-workers

**Decision.** One agent with JIT retrieval (M3), not an orchestrator with a worker pool.

**Alternatives considered.**
  - Orchestrator + 3 workers, one per document class.
  - Orchestrator + N workers scaled by document count.

**Reason.** Measured on 15 representative tasks: 3 workers scored 0.78 against the
single agent's 0.74 — four points, for 3.2x the cost. At an estimated value of
$1.10 per task and a ceiling of $0.18, the multi-agent version does not fit the
budget set at stage 1. The work is also only weakly parallel: document classes
share a lookup table, so workers duplicate roughly a third of their reads.

**What would reverse this.** Value per task rising above ~$4, or the corpus growing
past ~40 documents per task, where the single agent's context strategy starts to
degrade (currently holds to turn 60 with 8-14 documents).
```

### 04 · Build — 20%

A working agent on a real harness, with MCP integration where the domain warrants it. Every gate you
have passed applies: tools that survive the M2 rubric, a context strategy that holds at depth, errors
the agent can recover from, traces you can read.

### 05 · Harden — 15%

Threat model, with the lethal trifecta audit written out. Sandboxing, egress posture, credential
posture. Red-team your own agent and report what you found — including what you could not fix, and
what residual risk you are accepting.

An honest "here is the attack I could not defend against, and here is why I accept it" scores better
than a claim of completeness the panel can break in one question.

### 06 · Instrument — 10%

Traces, cost dashboard, alerting on agent-shaped signals (M10), and a documented failure taxonomy
**for this specific agent** — not the generic seven, but the ones you actually observed, with their
trace signatures.

### 07 · Ship and defend — 10%

Ten-minute demo, ten-minute panel defence.

## The three things that make this real

**A hard cost ceiling, set at stage 1 and enforced in CI.** You commit to a number per task before
you know whether you can hit it. If you cannot, you change the design, not the number — and if you do
change the number, that is a Decision Log entry explaining why the original estimate was wrong.

```yaml title=.github/workflows/capstone.yml
- name: Evals with the stage-1 cost gate
  run: |
    python -m evals.run \
      --tier easy,realistic \
      --repeats 3 \
      --min-pass-rate easy=1.0,realistic=0.85 \
      --max-usd-per-task 0.18 \
      --job-cost-cap 6.00
  # --max-usd-per-task is the ceiling committed at stage 1. Raising it is a
  # code change, reviewed like any other, and it requires a Decision Log entry.
  # --job-cost-cap stops the eval job itself becoming the incident.
```

**A required failure demo.** You will demonstrate your agent failing gracefully under a fault
**chosen by the panel, not by you**. A dependency that times out, a tool returning malformed data, a
budget exhausted mid-task. What we are looking for: does it degrade, escalate or abort as your design
says it should, and does the user learn something true about what happened?

**A scope change at day 5.** Your stakeholder will change a requirement. This is not hazing; it is
the single most reliable feature of real projects. Graded on how you handle it: what you renegotiate,
what you absorb, what you refuse, what you write down. A project plan that cannot survive one change
was not a plan.

## The defence panel

Two mandatory questions, known in advance, because knowing them in advance does not make them easier.

**"What happens at 10× traffic?"** Ten times the volume. Where does it break first — rate limits,
cost, latency, a downstream dependency, the human review queue? What is the actual bottleneck, and
what would you do? "It would probably be fine" is a failing answer; so is "I'd add more workers"
without a number.

**"How would this be attacked?"** Walk M11's three capabilities. What does it read that somebody else
could have written? What can it reach? What would stealing data look like, concretely, and what
stops it?

If your answer is "the model would refuse", you have not answered — that is a mitigation offered as a
control, and it is the exact error M11 exists to prevent.

Then whatever else the panel finds. The best defences are from students who already know their
system's weakest point and say so before being asked.

## Grading

| Dimension | Weight | What earns marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem fit | 20% | The right amount of autonomy, defended with the M4 framework |
| Engineering quality | 20% | Tools, context, errors, traces — the gates applied, not recited |
| Evaluation rigour | 25% | Real cases, validated judge, trajectory assertions, CI |
| Security posture | 20% | Threat model, hardening, honest residual risk |
| Decision Log judgement | 15% | Reasoning, alternatives, and what would change your mind |

Passing is 70% overall **and** a defence that survives both mandatory questions.

Note that evaluation rigour outweighs the build. That is not an accident, and it is the same claim
the course has been making since M4: the thing that separates a demo from a product is whether anyone
can tell if it works.

::: autopsy Delivered in public
Panel feedback is given in front of the cohort, to everyone's benefit. You will learn more from
watching six defences than from your own, because you will see six different ways of being wrong that
each looked completely reasonable from the inside.

The most common failure in this room is not a broken agent. It is an author who cannot say what their
agent is bad at.
:::

::: deeper If you are choosing your own problem
The best capstone problems share three properties, and it is worth checking yours against them
before day two rather than day nine.

**You understand the domain.** Not the agent part — the *work*. If you cannot say what a good answer
looks like, you cannot write the test set, and stage 2 is 20% of the grade.

**You can get real examples.** Twenty actual cases, from actual usage. A problem where you would have
to invent the test data is a problem where you will invent test data that flatters your agent (M8's
autopsy).

**It is small enough to finish and real enough to break.** A toy fails in no interesting way and
teaches nothing. Something ambitious runs out of time at stage 4 and never reaches hardening or
instrumentation — which are 25% between them.

A useful shape: one task, one kind of user, one integration, and genuine failure modes. "Reconcile
our invoices against purchase orders" beats "an assistant for the finance team" every time.
:::

## Where to go next

You now have a spine: build the loop, see it, evaluate it, harden it, cost it, ship it. Three habits
worth keeping:

**Keep the Observatory.** Every new agent gets traced from turn one. The habit of looking is the
whole difference.

**Keep writing evals first.** It stays uncomfortable and it stays correct.

**Keep the trifecta audit as a reflex.** Every new tool, every new connector, every new memory
directory. It takes thirty seconds and it is the highest-value thirty seconds in your review process.
