Ship one real thing

Everything so far has been scaffolded. Failing tests told you what to build; eval sets came pre-written; the security lab handed you the attacks. The capstone removes the scaffolding.

You will build one agent for one real problem, over two weeks, and defend it.

The idea in one line

Two students can build the same agent and receive very different grades. Judgement is weighted highest.

That is not a warning about strictness. It is the actual grading criterion. An ambitious agent with a weak eval suite and an unexamined threat model scores below a modest agent whose author can say exactly what it does badly, why they chose this architecture, and what would change their mind. The Decision Log carries more weight than the demo.

The seven stages, in order

The order is enforced, and the enforcement is the point. Stage 2 before stage 4 is what separates this from every project that shipped a demo and discovered its failure modes in production.

01 · Problem definition — 10%

Who is the user, what is the task, what does success mean, and why an agent? Apply the M4 framework in writing: enumerable steps, findings-dependence, value per task, reversibility, evaluability. Estimate the value of one successful task in money or time.

If the framework says "this should be a workflow", build the workflow. Choosing correctly against your own preference is worth more marks than building the more impressive thing.

02 · Eval-first design — 20%

Written and committed before a line of agent code exists. At least 30 cases across three tiers, outcome and trajectory assertions, a written judge rubric, and a validated judge — hand-label 30 outputs, measure kappa, iterate.

The heaviest-weighted stage, deliberately. This is where projects live or die, and doing it first is the only way to find out that you cannot define success — which is much better to discover in week one than in week two.

03 · Architecture — 15%

A diagram, plus Decision Log entries for: autonomy level (per action class, M13), tool design (M2), context strategy (M3), memory (M7), single versus multi-agent with the cost arithmetic (M9), and deployment shape (M15).

Each entry states the decision, the alternatives, the reason, and what evidence would reverse it. That last field is what makes it a decision log rather than a justification.

04 · Build — 20%

A working agent on a real harness, with MCP integration where the domain warrants it. Every gate you have passed applies: tools that survive the M2 rubric, a context strategy that holds at depth, errors the agent can recover from, traces you can read.

05 · Harden — 15%

Threat model, with the lethal trifecta audit written out. Sandboxing, egress posture, credential posture. Red-team your own agent and report what you found — including what you could not fix, and what residual risk you are accepting.

An honest "here is the attack I could not defend against, and here is why I accept it" scores better than a claim of completeness the panel can break in one question.

06 · Instrument — 10%

Traces, cost dashboard, alerting on agent-shaped signals (M10), and a documented failure taxonomy for this specific agent — not the generic seven, but the ones you actually observed, with their trace signatures.

07 · Ship and defend — 10%

Ten-minute demo, ten-minute panel defence.

The three things that make this real

A hard cost ceiling, set at stage 1 and enforced in CI. You commit to a number per task before you know whether you can hit it. If you cannot, you change the design, not the number — and if you do change the number, that is a Decision Log entry explaining why the original estimate was wrong.

A required failure demo. You will demonstrate your agent failing gracefully under a fault chosen by the panel, not by you. A dependency that times out, a tool returning malformed data, a budget exhausted mid-task. What we are looking for: does it degrade, escalate or abort as your design says it should, and does the user learn something true about what happened?

A scope change at day 5. Your stakeholder will change a requirement. This is not hazing; it is the single most reliable feature of real projects. Graded on how you handle it: what you renegotiate, what you absorb, what you refuse, what you write down. A project plan that cannot survive one change was not a plan.

The defence panel

Two mandatory questions, known in advance, because knowing them in advance does not make them easier.

"What happens at 10× traffic?" Ten times the volume. Where does it break first — rate limits, cost, latency, a downstream dependency, the human review queue? What is the actual bottleneck, and what would you do? "It would probably be fine" is a failing answer; so is "I'd add more workers" without a number.

"How would this be attacked?" Walk the trifecta. What does it read that an attacker can influence? What can it reach? What would exfiltration look like, concretely, and what stops it? If your answer is that the model would refuse, you have not answered.

Then whatever else the panel finds. The best defences are from students who already know their system's weakest point and say so before being asked.

Grading

DimensionWeightWhat earns marks
Problem fit20%The right amount of autonomy, defended with the M4 framework
Engineering quality20%Tools, context, errors, traces — the gates applied, not recited
Evaluation rigour25%Real cases, validated judge, trajectory assertions, CI
Security posture20%Threat model, hardening, honest residual risk
Decision Log judgement15%Reasoning, alternatives, and what would change your mind

Passing is 70% overall and a defence that survives both mandatory questions.

Note that evaluation rigour outweighs the build. That is not an accident, and it is the same claim the course has been making since M4: the thing that separates a demo from a product is whether anyone can tell if it works.

Delivered in public

Panel feedback is given in front of the cohort, to everyone's benefit. You will learn more from watching six defences than from your own, because you will see six different ways of being wrong that each looked completely reasonable from the inside.

The most common failure in this room is not a broken agent. It is an author who cannot say what their agent is bad at.

The whole thing

python -m lab16_capstone.checklist reports which stage artifacts exist and which gates pass — run it from day one, not day thirteen. It is deliberately a poor way to discover on day thirteen that stage 2 was never committed.

Where to go next

You now have a spine: build the loop, see it, evaluate it, harden it, cost it, ship it. Three habits worth keeping:

Keep the Observatory. Every new agent gets traced from turn one. The habit of looking is the whole difference.

Keep writing evals first. It stays uncomfortable and it stays correct.

Keep the trifecta audit as a reflex. Every new tool, every new connector, every new memory directory. It takes thirty seconds and it is the highest-value thirty seconds in your review process.
