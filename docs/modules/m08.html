The discipline that separates demos from products

Most agent projects that die do not die of a hard technical problem. They die because nobody could tell whether a change made things better, so changes stopped being made, and the thing that worked in the demo kept not working in production for reasons nobody could name.

The idea in one line

If you cannot evaluate it, you cannot improve it, and you should not ship it.

Why agent eval is harder

Evaluating a single model call is comparatively easy: one input, one output, compare to a reference. Agents break every one of those assumptions.

Multi-step. Failure can occur at any turn, and the observable failure is often several turns downstream of the actual one.

Stochastic. The same input produces different trajectories. A single run tells you almost nothing; you need repeats, and you need to look at variance.

Path-dependent. An early tool choice constrains everything after it. Two runs that both "fail" may have failed for unrelated reasons.

Many valid paths. Often several trajectories reach an equally good answer. An eval that asserts one specific path punishes correct behaviour and will be tuned away by whoever is trying to make the number go up.

Environmental. Agents touch real systems. Your eval needs a fixture environment that is deterministic enough to compare against and realistic enough to be worth comparing.

Outcome and trajectory

Outcome evaluation asks: is the final answer right? Necessary, insufficient.

Trajectory evaluation asks: did it get there sensibly? Did it call the tools it should have, avoid the ones it shouldn't, stay within a reasonable turn count, and not do anything alarming on the way?

You need both, because outcome-only evaluation cannot distinguish an agent that reasoned correctly from one that guessed. Consider a query with two plausible answers where the agent picks one at random and happens to be right. Outcome: pass. Trajectory: it never called the tool that disambiguates. That agent will fail tomorrow, and your eval suite just told you it was fine.

Note must_not_call and max_usd. An agent that gets the right answer by calling delete_and_recreate, or by spending four dollars, has not passed.

Start with twenty cases

The most common reason a team has no eval set is that they are waiting until they can build a good one. Do not wait.

Twenty real cases — drawn from actual usage, actual tickets, actual questions — is enough to catch the failures that matter. Prompt changes have been observed moving success rates from around 30% to around 80% at that sample size, because the first tranche of failures is usually systematic rather than subtle. You do not need statistical power to find a systematic bug; you need examples.

How to pick them:

  • Sample from reality, not from imagination. Your imagination produces the happy path.
  • Cover three tiers. Easy (should always pass — these catch regressions), realistic (the actual distribution), hard (should sometimes fail — these are your headroom).
  • Include the ones you are afraid of. The ambiguous request, the one where the right answer is "I don't know", the one where the correct action is to escalate.
  • Include cases that should fail closed. The agent should refuse, abstain or ask. If nothing in your suite rewards abstention, you will train it out.

Then grow the set from production failures. Every incident becomes a case. This is the flywheel.

The biases, and what to do about them

Position bias. In pairwise comparison, judges favour one position. Mitigate by running both orders and discarding disagreements, or by scoring absolutely rather than comparatively.

Verbosity bias. Longer, more fluent answers score higher regardless of substance. Mitigate with explicit length-independent criteria, and by including a padded-but-wrong case in your judge validation set.

Self-preference. Judges favour output from the same model family. Mitigate by using a different model as judge, or by anchoring scores to reference answers.

Leniency drift. Judges pass borderline cases. Mitigate by anchoring each score with a concrete example of what a 0 and a 3 look like.

Validate the judge

An unvalidated judge is a random number generator with good manners. Before you trust it:

  1. Hand-label 30 outputs yourself, against the rubric, before seeing the judge's labels.
  2. Run the judge on the same 30.
  3. Measure agreement — and measure Cohen's kappa, not raw agreement.
  4. Read every disagreement. The judge is usually right about something you under-specified.
  5. Fix the rubric. Re-measure. Repeat until kappa clears your threshold.

Raw agreement lies. On a set where 80% of cases pass, a judge that passes everything scores 80% and is worthless. Kappa corrects for chance agreement and exposes it.

And the rule that has to be stated because the temptation is real: when the judge and your labels disagree, you fix the rubric, never the labels. Adjusting the reference to match the judge is how teams produce a suite that agrees with itself and nothing else.

Human evaluation is irreplaceable

Sit down and read fifty transcripts. Not summaries — transcripts.

You will find things no automated eval was looking for, because automated evals only check what someone thought to assert. The canonical example: an agent whose answers were factually correct and well-cited, passing every check, which had quietly learned to prefer SEO content farms over authoritative sources — because the farms were easier to extract clean quotes from. No rubric dimension existed for source quality until a human noticed. Then it became one.

Budget for this. Every significant change, before it ships.

Evals in CI

An eval suite that runs when someone remembers is not a control.

Four things this needs to work in practice.

Handle stochasticity honestly. Run each case N times and set the threshold on the aggregate. A single flaky failure must not block a merge, and a case that passes 2 of 5 times is failing even though it is green sometimes. Report pass rates, not pass/fail.

Cap the cost of the eval job itself. An eval suite that can spend unboundedly on a PR is its own incident. Cap it, and fail the job if the cap is hit.

Tier what blocks the merge. Easy tier must be 100% — any failure is a regression. Realistic tier blocks below a threshold. Hard tier is reported, never blocking, or people will delete the hard cases.

Track cost and turn count as first-class metrics. A change that keeps accuracy and doubles cost is a regression. If your CI only reports accuracy, that regression ships.

Variance is the one that gets left out. An agent that is right 90% of the time with low variance is a product. One that averages 90% by being brilliant on Mondays and useless on Tuesdays is a support burden, and the mean hides it entirely.

Self-improvement, and the trap

Feed failing transcripts back to a model with the tool definitions and prompts, and ask what went wrong and what to change. It is remarkably effective — reported efficiency gains in the region of 40% — for the same reason as in M2: it is the reader you are writing for.

The trap is overfitting. Iterating against the same twenty cases eventually produces a system tuned to those twenty cases. Guard it: hold out cases the improvement loop never sees, refresh the set from production regularly, and treat a suspiciously large jump as evidence of leakage rather than success.

The 95% agent that failed in production

Passing 95% in the suite, visibly bad in the wild. The suite had been written by the team, from memory, in an afternoon — which meant it encoded the questions they expected. Every case was well-formed, unambiguous, answerable from one document, and phrased the way an engineer phrases things.

Real traffic was none of those. Users asked compound questions, referenced things not in the corpus, phrased requests ambiguously, and frequently wanted an answer the sources could not support. The agent had never been evaluated on abstention, so it did what it had been optimised to do: answer confidently.

The fix was procedural, not technical. Sample eval cases from actual logs, stratified — including the messy, the ambiguous and the unanswerable — and add every production failure to the set as a permanent case.
